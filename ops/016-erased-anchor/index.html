01

Intel Brief

CONTEXT

MITRE ATT&CK T1070.009: adversaries clear artifacts of previously established persistence, removing services, deleting executables, deleting accounts, to deny defenders the evidence of how the implant kept coming back. This file runs that clearing end to end, then detects it from a manifest that predates the attacker. Two actors: the attacker, who owns the lab directory, the persistence artifacts, and the shred; and the defender, who owns the manifest reads, the restore, and every inventory in the blue phase.

Why this technique. Persistence is an anchor, and an anchor is evidence. While the implant is running, the attacker wants the anchor: the cron entry that relaunches it every five minutes, the service unit that restarts it, the binary on disk. Once the implant has moved on, or once the defender is closing in, the anchor becomes a liability. Every persistence artifact is a labeled arrow pointing at the attacker's method. Clearing them removes the arrow. The defender still finds the intrusion, but the how-it-kept-coming-back is gone, and without it the defender cannot scope the incident, cannot be sure the implant is really dead, and cannot harden the right door.

Why not hide the persistence instead. The obvious alternative is hiding inside the listing: dotfiles, deep paths, the T1564.001 ground from GH-009. Break it on the concrete input: a sha256 inventory still lists the files, because the artifacts exist on disk for any recursive sweep. Hiding only beats a casual glance. The attacker here needs the artifact to not exist at all, because the defender's question is not "what is in this directory" but "what was here, with hashes". Clearing removes the artifact entirely, at the cost of the directory-mtime scar and the manifest gap, which the blue phase reads.

Why not timestomp the artifacts into the background. The other obvious alternative is timestomping the files into the past, the T1070.006 ground from GH-005. Break it: you cannot timestomp a file you need gone. The bytes remain on disk for carving, and the persistence mechanism itself still works, which is the opposite of the attacker's goal once the anchor must die. Clearing is for when the persistence has done its job and the implant has moved on. Timestamps hide a file's story; clearing removes the file.

Why not clear the command history. The third alternative is clearing the shell history, the T1070.003 ground from GH-001, to hide the clearing commands. Break it: the history covers the attacker's keystrokes, not the missing files. The manifest gap is independent of what was typed. A defender with a manifest that predates the attacker does not need the attacker's keystrokes. The history is the wrong evidence to protect, which is exactly why this technique and GH-001 are different files.

The mechanism, in one line. Persistence is a mechanism that runs the implant again after reboot or on schedule: a cron entry, a service unit, a startup script. Indicator removal is deleting or modifying artifacts the adversary's own activity created, to remove evidence of the presence or hinder defenses. A baseline manifest is a sha256 inventory of the known-good state, taken before the attacker arrives: the independent observation path, because it describes what should be here regardless of what is here now. A directory mtime is a directory's modification timestamp, updated whenever entries are added or removed: the scar deletions leave, because removing a file changes its parent directory. Terms earned, no borrowing.

The concrete case, before the abstraction. Three artifacts live in /tmp/gh016/persist: cron.d/persist launches the implant every five minutes, units/persist.service is the unit stand-in, bin/implant.sh is the inert payload, never executed. The defender's manifest holds all three hashes. shred -u removes all three, overwriting before unlinking. After: ls -R shows empty directories, grep finds no gh016 strings anywhere. The defender's manifest check reports FAILED open or read on all three. One shred, three empty directories, one manifest that refuses to agree.

Scope. T1070.009 covers clearing persistence broadly: removing services, deleting executables, modifying registries and plists, deleting accounts. This file works the Linux service-and-binary case end to end: the cron entry, the unit, the executable, the shred, the manifest detection, the backup restore, the permission gate, and the scheduled inventory. The lab runs in a disposable namespace because the attack destroys evidence by design; the backup and the manifest are the defender's earlier inventory, made before the attacker acts.

Build the lab

The three artifacts go in through heredocs. cron.d/persist is the cron.d-format entry launching the implant every five minutes. units/persist.service is the systemd unit stand-in. bin/implant.sh is the stand-in for the payload the persistence would launch: kept, never run. Then the defender's earlier inventory, made before the attacker: cp -r the known-good copies into backup, and sha256sum writes the manifest. Phase Red: erase the anchor

ATTACK

First the before view, then the shred, then the attacker's confirmation that enumeration finds nothing.

1

The attack

shred -u overwrites all three artifacts before unlinking them: the cron entry, the unit, the implant binary. shred is silent on success: the evidence is what enumeration says after. ls -R shows empty directories, with the (empty directories) marker. grep -ri gh016 finds no strings anywhere under persist. Phase Blue: read the manifest

DEFENSE

The defender does not trust the listing. The defender reads the manifest that predates the attacker: detect from the independent inventory, record before touching, restore from the known-good backup, then gate the locations and inventory the manifest on a schedule.

Here is the trap: running ls, seeing empty directories, and calling the persistence locations clean. That is trusting the same enumeration the attacker just emptied. The detection is the manifest check: sha256sum -c against the known-good inventory, while the clearing is still in effect.

1

Then the scar: stat -c '%y %n' on the persist directories shows the mtimes moved when the entries were removed, even though the directories read empty. 4

PREVENTION: the permission gate

chmod 755 on the persist directories, root-owned: only root writes the persistence locations. The demonstration: su -s /bin/sh nobody tries the attacker's rm as the unprivileged user, and the kernel refuses it: rm: cannot remove '/tmp/gh016/persist/cron.d/persist': Permission denied. The gate raises the required privilege to root before the technique can run. D3FEND D3-SCP: System Configuration Permissions names exactly this shape: restricting system configuration modifications to the principals that need them.

bash
$ echo "--- PREVENTION: the permission gate ---"; chmod 755 /tmp/gh016/persist /tmp/gh016/persist/cron.d /tmp/gh016/persist/units /tmp/gh016/persist/bin; su -s /bin/sh nobody -c 'rm /tmp/gh016/persist/cron.d/persist' 2>&1; echo "(refused: only root writes the persistence locations)"

06

Debrief

TAKEAWAY

When the listing says clean and the manifest says cleared, the manifest is the truth: inventory what was here, not what is here.

IOC checklist: signs this technique was used

  • Persistence locations that read empty but whose directory mtimes moved with no change record
  • A baseline manifest reporting FAILED open or read on service, timer, or launcher artifacts
  • Audit events showing shred or repeated overwrites run against persistence paths
  • A cron entry or service unit present in backups but absent on the live host
  • A known implant whose launch mechanism cannot be found anywhere on the box

Reproducibility checklist
